Chapter 1

What is the disorder called?

The disorder is called albinism. It is a group of genetic conditions where the body makes little or no melanin, the pigment that colours skin, hair and eyes.

People with albinism come from every background. Skin, hair and eye colour vary from person to person. (Illustration)

Where the name comes from

“Albinism” comes from the Latin word albus, meaning white. The name is a little misleading, because many people with albinism have cream, yellow, light brown or even reddish hair and skin rather than pure white. The respectful way to talk about someone is “a person with albinism”, rather than using the older word “albino” as a label.

Types and medical names

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA)

The most common type. It affects the skin, hair and eyes. There are at least seven types, OCA1 to OCA7, named after the gene involved.

Ocular albinism (OA)

A rarer type that mainly affects the eyes. Skin and hair usually look similar to the rest of the person’s family.

Rare syndromes such as Hermansky-Pudlak and Chediak-Higashi syndrome also include albinism, along with other health problems.

How common is it?

About 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people worldwide have albinism. It is found in every ethnic group, and is more common in some regions. In southern Africa it affects around 1 in 4,000 people.

Word to know: melanin
The natural pigment made by special skin cells that gives colour to skin, hair and eyes, and helps the eyes develop before birth.

Where melanin is made

Melanin is made in the middle layer of the skin. Less melanin means paler skin, hair and eyes.
Chapter 2

How is albinism diagnosed?

Albinism is usually noticed at birth or in early infancy, then confirmed with an eye exam and, often, a genetic test.

The steps doctors take

1

Looking at skin, hair and eye colour. A doctor compares the child’s colouring with their family’s. Very pale skin or hair can be the first clue.

2

A full eye exam. An eye doctor (ophthalmologist) checks for nystagmus, light-coloured or see-through irises, an under-developed fovea, and how well the eyes focus. Eye findings are present in every type of albinism.

3

Family history. Questions about relatives with albinism or eye problems help show how it may be inherited.

4

Genetic testing. A blood or saliva sample is checked for changes in the genes linked to albinism. This confirms the type and helps families understand the chance of passing it on.

Why the type matters
Knowing the exact type helps doctors know whether to look for other health problems, such as the bleeding or infection risks seen in some rare syndromes.
Chapter 3

How does a person get albinism?

Albinism is caused by a change (mutation) in a gene, and in nearly all cases that change is inherited from parents. It cannot be caught, and nothing a parent did causes it.

A gene mutation, passed down

Several genes (including TYR, OCA2, TYRP1, SLC45A2 and GPR143) give the body instructions for making melanin. A mutation in one of them stops it working properly. Because the mutation is in the DNA of every cell, including egg and sperm cells, it can be handed from parent to child. Very rarely a mutation can appear for the first time in a child.

Autosomal recessive inheritance

Most types of OCA need two changed copies of a gene, one from each parent. A parent with just one changed copy is a carrier, and usually has no signs of albinism. Many families learn they are carriers only after a child is born with albinism.

Chances for each child when both parents carry one changed copy of an OCA gene.

X-linked inheritance

The most common type of ocular albinism is carried on the X chromosome. It mostly affects males and is usually passed on by mothers who are carriers.

Chapter 4

Main signs and symptoms

Albinism affects colouring, the eyes and sun sensitivity. It does not affect intelligence or lifespan in most types.

Skin and hair

  • Very light skin, which burns easily and may freckle instead of tanning
  • White, cream, yellow, light brown or reddish hair
  • Higher risk of sunburn and, over time, skin cancer

Eyes and vision

  • Nystagmus: small, involuntary eye movements
  • Photophobia: discomfort or pain in bright light
  • Reduced sharpness of vision, often not fully fixed by glasses
  • Strabismus: eyes that turn inward or outward
  • Light blue, grey or brown eyes, with irises that let light through
  • Trouble with depth perception, or an unusual head tilt to see better
With little melanin in the iris, extra light enters the eye, which is why bright light is uncomfortable.
Chapter 5

What is it like to have albinism?

Most people with albinism go to school, work, play sport and have families like everyone else. A few adjustments make everyday life easier.

Hats, sunglasses and tinted lenses are everyday tools. (Illustration)

Seeing the world

Vision is blurry rather than dark. Many people hold things close, sit near the front of the classroom, or use a magnifier or screen zoom. Faces across a room can be hard to recognise, and bright sunshine can feel painful.

Sun and skin

Planning ahead is part of daily life: sunscreen, protective clothing and shade. Outdoor activities are still possible, often at cooler times of day.

Being noticed

Looking different can attract stares, nicknames or questions. Some people feel confident, others find it tiring, and talking with others with albinism often helps. In some countries, myths lead to real discrimination and even violence, so awareness and support matter.

The important point
Albinism changes how a person sees and how they handle the sun. It does not change who they are or what they can achieve.
Chapter 6

Is there treatment?

There is no cure for albinism, because the gene change cannot yet be reversed. But symptoms can be managed well, especially when care starts early.

Vision care

Regular eye exams, glasses or contact lenses, tinted lenses and sunglasses, magnifiers, large-print and screen-reading tools. Some children have surgery for strabismus. A few people use medication or surgery for nystagmus, with mixed results.

Skin protection

Broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30 or higher), long sleeves, wide-brimmed hats, shade, and a skin check by a dermatologist at least once a year. Skin cancer found early is usually very treatable.

Support

Help in school (large print, seating, extra time), genetic counselling for families, and support groups for emotional wellbeing.

Research

Scientists are studying gene therapy and other approaches, but none are standard treatments yet.

Chapter 7

Other important things to know

Myths and facts

Myth: People with albinism have red eyes. Fact: Eyes are usually blue, grey or brown. They can look reddish in some light.

Myth: Albinism is contagious. Fact: It is genetic and cannot spread between people.

Myth: Body parts of people with albinism hold magic powers. Fact: This is false and dangerous. People have been attacked because of this belief, mainly in parts of East Africa.

Albinism in animals

Albinism also occurs in animals, such as snakes, rabbits and alligators. An animal with albinism has pink or red eyes, whereas an animal that is simply white (called leucistic) keeps dark eyes.

International Albinism Awareness Day

The United Nations marks 13 June each year to raise awareness and protect the rights of people with albinism.

Ten interesting facts

1

About 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people worldwide have albinism.

2

It affects people of every ethnic group and in every country.

3

The name comes from the Latin albus, meaning “white”.

4

Hair can be white, yellow, light brown or even reddish, not only white.

5

Every type of albinism affects the eyes and vision.

6

The eyes are usually blue, grey or brown, not red.

7

Two carrier parents have a 1 in 4 chance of having a child with albinism with each pregnancy.

8
