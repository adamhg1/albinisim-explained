Chapter 1

What is albinism?

Albinism is a group of genetic conditions where the body makes little or no melanin — the pigment that gives colour to your skin, hair and eyes.

📷 Figure 1.1: Visual Explanatory Panel
Skin, hair and eyes are much lighter when the body lacks natural melanin pigment.

It all comes down to melanin

Melanin is a natural colouring made by special cells. It decides how dark your skin, hair, and eyes are, and helps your eyes develop correctly before birth. Albinism is not a disease, you cannot catch it, and it never changes how smart a person is.

How common is it?

About 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people globally have albinism. It affects every race and nation on Earth.

The Two Main Types:

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA): The most common form. It affects the skin, hair, and eyes together.

Ocular albinism (OA): A rarer form that mostly affects the eyes, while skin and hair look normal.

Chapter 2

How is albinism diagnosed?

Albinism is usually noticed very early by doctors or parents — often the moment a baby is born due to very pale skin and white hair.

📷 Figure 2.1: Diagnostic Observation
Initial checkups monitor skin pigmentation values and involuntary muscle movements.

What the eye doctor looks for

  • Nystagmus: Fast, involuntary side-to-side eye movements.
  • Photophobia: Extreme sensitivity to bright light and sun glare.
  • Strabismus: Eyes that cross or point in different directions.
Chapter 3

How can a person get albinism?

Albinism is an inherited genetic condition. You cannot catch it from someone else, and nothing in the environment causes it.

Most parents are carriers

Albinism is an autosomal recessive condition. This means a child must inherit two copies of the changed gene (one from each parent) to have the condition. Parents are usually just carriers, meaning they carry the gene copy but show no signs themselves.

Chapter 4

Signs, symptoms and daily life

People with albinism lead full, active lives, but they must adapt to their environment to protect their eyes and skin day-to-day.

Everyday Habits: Wearing sunscreens daily, using wide-brimmed hats, sitting near the front of classrooms, and using high-contrast large print text.
Chapter 5

What treatment is there?

There is no cure for albinism, and no medicine can force the body to create melanin. Instead, treatment focuses on management:

  • Prescription glasses or contacts to optimize low-vision focus.
  • Consistent application of SPF 30+ sunscreens to avoid burns.
  • Regular skin evaluations by dermatologists to watch for changes.
Chapter 6

Looking after eyes & skin

Proactive physical maintenance prevents long-term cell damage. Protecting the eyes cuts down on severe fatigue, and monitoring UV rays prevents serious skin conditions later in life.

Chapter 7

Community and support

No family has to go through this alone. There are global support systems that provide excellent community guidance:

  • NOAH: National Organization for Albinism and Hypopigmentation.
  • Albinism Fellowship: Group active across the UK and Ireland.
Chapter 8

More interesting facts

Here are a few quick informational items regarding the condition:

  • Albinism occurs naturally across the animal kingdom, including birds and marine life.
  • The term comes from the Latin word "albus", which translates directly to "white".
  • June 13 is officially observed as International Albinism Awareness Day.
