Chapter 1

What is albinism?

Albinism is a group of genetic conditions where the body makes little or no melanin — the pigment that gives colour to your skin, hair and eyes.

Skin, hair, and eyes are much lighter when the body makes very little melanin.

It all comes down to melanin

Melanin is the natural colouring made by special cells in your skin. It decides how dark your skin, hair and eyes are, and it also helps your eyes develop before you are born. When the body makes very little melanin, or none at all, those colours become much lighter. That is albinism.

Albinism is not a disease. You cannot catch it, it does not get worse over time, and it does not change how clever someone is.

How common is it?

About 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people around the world have albinism. It happens in every ethnic group and in every country. In parts of Africa it is more common — roughly 1 in 4,000 people in southern Africa.

The two main types

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA)

The most common type. It affects the skin, the hair and the eyes. There are several forms, and the colouring can be anything from very pale to light brown.

Ocular albinism (OA)

A rarer type that mostly affects the eyes. The skin and hair can look almost the same as the rest of the family's.

Skin Structure Layers

Melanin is made in the middle layer of the skin. Less melanin means paler skin, hair and eyes.
Word to know: melanin
Melanin is the pigment that colours your skin, hair and eyes. It also protects your skin from the sun and helps your eyes develop properly.

How is albinism diagnosed?

Albinism is usually noticed very early — often the moment a baby is born.
