Chapter 1

What is the disorder called?

The disorder is called albinism. It is a group of genetic conditions where the body makes little or no melanin, the pigment that colours skin, hair and eyes.

A girl with albinism from Papua New Guinea. Photo: Muntuwandi, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Where the name comes from

“Albinism” comes from the Latin word albus, meaning white. The name is a little misleading, because many people with albinism have cream, yellow, light brown or even reddish hair and skin rather than pure white. The respectful way to talk about someone with albinism is to say “a person with albinism”, instead of using the word "albino" as a label.

Types and medical names

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA)

This is the most common type. Due to the lack of melanin produced in teh body, it affects the skin, hair and eyes of a person. There are at seven main types, named OCA1- OCA7. Each type gets it's name from the gene involved.

Ocular albinism (OA)

A rarer type that mainly affects the eyes. Skin and hair usually look similar to the rest of the person’s family.

Rare syndromes such as Hermansky-Pudlak and Chediak-Higashi syndrome also include albinism, along with other health problems.

How common is it?

About 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people worldwide have albinism. It is found in every ethnic group, and is more common in some regions. In southern Africa it affects around 1 in 4,000 people.

Word to know: melanin
The natural pigment made by special skin cells that gives colour to skin, hair and eyes, and helps the eyes develop before birth.

Where melanin is made

Layers of the epidermis, the top part of the skin. Melanin is made by cells called melanocytes in its deepest layer. Diagram: Serephine, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
Chapter 2

How is albinism diagnosed?

Albinism is usually noticed early in life. It is then confirmed with an eye exam and, often, a genetic test.

The steps doctors take

1

Looking at skin, hair and eye colour. A doctor may look at a child's skin, hair and eye colour and compare it with their family's. Very light skin and hair can be a first sign.

2

A full eye exam. An eye doctor (ophthalmologist) checks for uncontroled eye moments, light-coloured irises, an under-developed fovea. They may also test how well the eyes focus. Eye abnomalties can be found in every type of albinism.

3

Family history. Questions about relatives with albinism or eye problems help show how it may be inherited.

4

Genetic testing. A blood or saliva sample is checked for changes in the genes linked to albinism. This confirms the type and helps families understand the chance of passing it on.

Why the type matters
Knowing the exact type helps doctors know whether to look for other health problems, such as the bleeding or infection risks seen in some rare syndromes.
Chapter 3

How does a person get albinism?

Albinism is caused by a mutation in a gene, and in nearly all cases that mutation is inherited from parents.

A gene mutation, passed down

Several genes (including TYR, OCA2, TYRP1, SLC45A2 and GPR143) give the body instructions for making melanin. A mutation in one of them stops it working properly. Since the mutation is in the DNA of every cell, including egg and sperm cells, it can be handed from parent to child. Very rarely a mutation can appear for the first time in a child.

Autosomal recessive inheritance

Most types of OCA need two changed copies of a gene, one from each parent. A parent with just one changed copy is a carrier, and usually has no signs of albinism. Many families learn they are carriers only after a child is born with albinism.

X-linked inheritance

The most common type of ocular albinism is carried on the X chromosome. It mostly affects males and is usually passed on by mothers who are carriers.

Chapter 4

Main signs and symptoms

Albinism affects colouring, the eyes and sun sensitivity. It does not affect intelligence or lifespan in most types.

Skin and hair

  • Very light skin, which burns easily and may freckle instead of tanning
  • White, cream, yellow, light brown or reddish hair
  • Higher risk of sunburn and, over time, skin cancer

Eyes and vision

  • Nystagmus: small, involuntary eye movements
  • Photophobia: discomfort or pain in bright light
  • Reduced sharpness of vision, often not fully fixed by glasses
  • Strabismus: eyes that turn inward or outward
  • Light blue, grey or brown eyes, with irises that let light through
  • Trouble with depth perception, or an unusual head tilt to see better
Chapter 5

What is it like to have albinism?

Most people with albinism go to school, work, play sports.

A girl with albinism from Honduras. Photo via Wikipedia (uploaded by Muntuwandi), CC BY-SA 2.5. Many people with albinism use hats, sunglasses and sunscreen outdoors.

Seeing the world

Vision is blurry rather than dark. Many people hold things close and sit near the front of the classroom, to better support their vision. Faces across a room can be hard to recognise, and bright sunshine can feel painful.

Sun and skin

Planning ahead is part of daily life: sunscreen, protective clothing and shade is important becuase people wiht albinism are more vulnerable to sunburn and damage.

Being noticed

People with albinism may attrat stares and quesetions. While some people feel confindent, others may find it tiring or draining. By raising awareness, it can help create a inclusive environment.

The important point
Albinism changes how a person sees and how they handle the sun. It does not change who they are or what they can achieve.
Chapter 6

Is there treatment?

There is no cure for albinism, because the gene change cannot yet be reversed. However, symptoms can be managed well, especially when care starts early.

Vision care

Vision care can include regular eye exams, glasses/ sunglasses or contact lenses, and large-print reading tools. Some children have surgery for eye alignment (strabismus), which causes double vision or lazy eye. In some cases, people use medication or surgery to help manage nystagmus.

Skin protection

People with albinism take extra care to use broad-spectrum sunscreen, long sleeves, wide-brimmed hats, shade, and attend dermatologist appointments. Skin cancer found early is usually very treatable.

Support

Support can be found in schools as they provide large print, seating, extra time, during tests.

Research

Scientists are studying gene therapy and other approaches, however, none are standard treatments yet.

Chapter 7

Other important things to know

Myths and facts

Myth: People with albinism have red eyes. Fact: Eyes are usually blue, grey or brown. They can look reddish in some light.

Myth: Albinism is contagious. Fact: It is genetic and cannot spread between people.

Myth: Body parts of people with albinism hold magic powers. Fact: This is false and dangerous. People have been attacked because of this belief, mainly in parts of East Africa.

Albinism in animals

Albinism also occurs in animals, such as snakes, rabbits and alligators. An animal with albinism has pink or red eyes, whereas an animal that are white (called leucistic) keeps dark eyes.

International Albinism Awareness Day

The United Nations has dedicated a day each year, June 13 to raise awareness and protect the rights of people with albinism.
